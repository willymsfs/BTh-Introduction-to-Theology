Chapter 1Foundations

What Is Theology?

Three positions on God, three positions on whether we can speak of God at all, and eight attempts — none of them final — to define the discipline that results.

Leitmotif — the Burning Bush (Ex 3:2–6). The fire that burns and is not consumed opens the God-question, returns at the hinge into the definitions, and seals the close: "I will turn aside and see this great sight" (Ex 3:3) — theology begins in that turning aside, and ends, as Moses' encounter ended, in a sending: "Come, I will send you" (Ex 3:10).

A. Can God's existence be assumed?

Theology = theos + logos, God-talk — but in a world of secularism, agnosticism and atheism, existence cannot be taken for granted. Three positions divide the field: the Atheistic View (Camus, Marx, Nietzsche, Freud — the problem of evil, God as projection); the Agnostic View (the question itself is not, after all, important); and the Theistic View, which the chapter endorses — God as Ultimate Ground of Being, knowable by natural reason. Aquinas's Five Ways move from effect to cause to Prime Mover, but as Newman cautioned, such proofs offer only "a convergence of diverse probabilities," not coercive certainty. Reason opens a path to God; it does not compel the traveller to walk it.

B. Can finite language reach an infinite God?

Even granting existence, can language reach the Ineffable? The classical Hindu neti neti ("not this, not this") and mystical negative theology both deny the possibility. The biblical tradition affirms a limited but real positive knowledge, mediated through three ways of using words about God: Univocal (the same word, the same meaning — impossible for God-talk, since God is Infinite and the creature finite); Equivocal (the same word, unrelated meanings — would collapse theology into nonsense); and Analogical (a word applying by proportion and participation, developed by Aristotle and refined by Aquinas). Calling God "our Father" is neither univocal nor equivocal — we purify the earthly notion of its limits and apply it to God in an eminent way. Analogy alone makes the whole theological enterprise possible.

Indian context

The Upanishadic instinct toward silence before Brahman and the biblical confidence that God has a name and a face in Jesus Christ are held together, not resolved by choosing one over the other. Analogical knowledge is precisely this middle path.

C. God-experience and the language of symbol

Religious experience — the Burning Bush, the Transfiguration, Emmaus — can only be expressed symbolically: "Theology uses ordinary language in an extraordinary way." Jesus Christ is the supreme symbol, not merely pointing to God but God-Incarnate; the Eucharist both signifies and is the presence it mediates.

D. Eight definitions — a series, not a sentence

The chapter refuses a single tidy definition, stacking eight instead, each adding what the others leave out:

  1. Theologia — theos + logos; from Plato's critique of the poets to the medieval university discipline.
  2. Faith Seeking Understanding (Anselm) — fides quaerens intellectum, balanced between Fideism and Rationalism.
  3. Lex Orandi, Lex Credendi (the Eastern Way) — the law of praying is the law of believing; liturgy as locus theologicus.
  4. Theological Anthropology (Rahner) — "whatever we say about God says something about us... God and humanity are correlative terms."
  5. Critical Reflection on Praxis (Gutierrez) — the shift from orthodoxy alone to orthopraxis.
  6. Theology as Hermeneutics — continuous reinterpretation of the past in dialogue with the present, against nihilism, relativism and fundamentalism.
  7. Interpretation of the Christian Story (mythos) — symbols, rituals and narratives that mediate the divine presence.
  8. The Working Definition — systematic, critical, rooted in Revelation, open to reinterpretation, and ecclesial.

F. Theology and other sciences

Philosophy was long the ancilla theologiae, theology's handmaid — Platonic categories for Origen and Augustine, Aristotelian for Aquinas, Existentialist for Rahner. Indian context: Upadhyaya, Abhishiktananda and Panikkar used Vedanta, understanding the Trinity through Saccidananda — an Indian ancilla theologiae placed at the service of the Christian mystery exactly as the Greek Fathers once placed the Logos.

G. Eight goals of theologizing

To awaken the Christ-experience; to explain and systematize faith; to sift Scripture for the Word of God today; to interpret prophetically for the present age; to expose the hierarchy of truths; to serve pastoral formation; to serve the Church's mission and witness; and to work toward the Kingdom of God.

Chapter 2Foundations

The Sources of Theology

Chapter 1 asked what theology is. Chapter 2 asks where the theologian stands while doing it — foundations, sources and resources are together the holy ground beneath every theological sentence.

The Hinge. "Come no closer; remove your sandals, for the place where you are standing is holy ground" (Ex 3:5). The voice that sent Moses to the cry of the people (Ex 3:7, 10) did not fall silent when the canon closed — Sources hand the fire on; Resources are where the same voice still speaks.

A. Faith and Revelation — the foundations

Two sides of one event: Revelation is God's self-communication; Faith is the human reception and response. Faith and belief are not the same — fides qua (faith as trust) grounds fides quae (faith's conceptual content), which is why one Christ can generate different Christologies without dividing the one faith.

Four models of Revelation

Doctrine (cognitive propositions) · Presence (personal encounter) · Experience (universal existential openness) · History (a public event open to investigation) — not exclusive, but dimensions of one reality.

B. From one Tradition to three Sources

Vatican II resolved a centuries-long debate — Luther and Calvin's Sola Scriptura against Trent's "Scripture and unwritten traditions," hardened after Trent into a defective Two-Source Theory. There is, in fact, one source, the Apostolic Tradition, concretely identified through three linked realities, "so linked and joined together that one cannot stand without the others" (Dei Verbum, 10):

  • Sacred Scripture — norma normans non normata, the norm which norms all else and is itself normed by no other criterion.
  • Tradition and traditions — six vehicles: different ecclesial traditions (22 Individual Churches; India holds two, Syro-Malabar and Syro-Malankara); liturgy, sacraments and prayers (the Chapter 1 callback: lex orandi, lex credendi); creeds (Apostles', Nicene, Constantinopolitan); the Fathers of the Church (Antiquity, Orthodoxy, Holiness of Life, Recognition); dogmas (standing in a hierarchy of truths); and customs, disciplines, codes and catechisms, from Trent (1566) to the CCC (1992).
  • Magisterium — the college of bishops in communion with the Pope.

C. Resources — where God still speaks

Where the Sources are the normative, historically fixed media of the one Tradition, the Resources are the wider field read through the Signs of the Times: people and their experience, cultural resources, other religious traditions, peoples' movements and the voice of the marginalized, and the cry of the poor — none of them a normative Source, all of them places God speaks today.

"God speaks today in a very special way through the cry of the poor, their struggles, aspirations, visions and hopes."

Chapter 3History

Development and Pluralism in Theology

Why is there not just one theology, but many, across the Bible, the early Church, the Middle Ages, and today? Five historical periods, walked in order.

A. Pluralism is not Relativism

Pluralism means one truth approached from more than one legitimate angle; relativism denies a single truth exists at all — "a negation of realism." Five kinds of legitimate difference: Fundamental, Speculative, Biblical, Magisterial, and Contextual theologies.

B. Theologies in the Bible

The Gospels were written backwards: the earliest preaching was the bare kerygma (Christ died and rose), from which grew the Passion Narrative, and only later the material on Jesus's earlier ministry placed in front of it. Mark begins with the adult Jesus and ends at the empty tomb — no birth narrative, because Mark's purpose was to preach salvation, not write biography. Result: a Matthean, a Marcan, a Lucan and a Johannine theology — four portraits of one Christ.

C. The Fathers of the Church

Four qualities: soundness of doctrine, holiness of life, official recognition, antiquity. Three geographic streams — Semitic (Ephraem at Edessa), Greek (Alexandria, culminating in the Cappadocians), Latin (Rome and Carthage, culminating in Augustine). Indian context: the Syro-Malabar and Syro-Malankara Churches inherit their theological ancestry directly from the Semitic-Syriac stream.

Twelve developments in this period: fixing the NT Canon · the role of Tradition against Gnosticism · the ecumenical Creeds · Chalcedon's Christology (Alexandrian vs Antiochene) · East/West approaches to the Trinity · the rise of the five Patriarchates · sacramentality (Augustine against the Donatists) · grace (Augustine against Pelagius) · the beginnings of biblical hermeneutics (Origen) · theology meeting Greek culture (the Logos) · first attempts to relate reason and faith (the Cappadocians) · the rise of theological schools (Alexandria, Antioch).

D. Scholastic Theology

Four periods, 800–1500 AD, organized around university lectures and disputations. Two questions dominate: the proper role of reason, and how to systematize doctrine. Gilson's phrase: "cathedrals of the mind." Six figures trace the spectrum: Anselm (1033–1109), faith seeking understanding, father of Scholasticism; Bernard (1091–1153), mystical theology, "to know Jesus and Him crucified"; Aquinas (1225–1274), synthesis emphasising the intellect; Bonaventure (1221–1274), synthesis emphasising the will; Scotus (1266–1308), a Christ-centred synthesis, the Incarnation willed independently of sin; and Ockham (1285–1347), nominalism and Ockham's Razor, the breakdown of the synthesis.

E. The Post-Scholastic Era

The Reformation split the Church; Trent answered defensively. Three later currents: the Neo-Scholastic Renewal (Leo XIII's Aeterni Patris, 1879); the Existentialist Thrust (Gabriel Marcel's problem/mystery, having/being); and the Kairos of Vatican II, where Congar, Daniélou, de Lubac and Rahner — all trained in the Neo-Scholastic mould — opened it up from within.

F. Contextual Theologies

Three roots: Gospel, Church, Culture. Named instances: Continental/National theologies · Secularisation (Bonhoeffer) · Death of God theology · Black theology · Feminist theologies · Ecofeminism · Dalit Theology (India) · Minjung Theology (Korea, from han, repressed anger). A necessary caution: not everything calling itself contextual is automatically authentically Catholic — tested against the criteria of Chapter 5.

Chapter 4Models

Selected Models of Theology

Seven theologians, seven ways faith and culture are actually balanced in practice — presented in historical order, two of them rooted directly in Indian ecclesial reality.

Four ways to strike the balance of faith and culture

Translation Model — hands on the inherited tradition. Anthropological Model — focuses on the local cultural context. Synthetic Model — tradition and context, roughly equal. Transcendental Model — focuses on the theologizing subject itself.

Indian context: two of these seven are chosen precisely for India — Ephraem, root of the Syro-Malabar and Syro-Malankara traditions, and Schmemann's celebrative liturgical model, shared with all Oriental traditions. Two more arise directly from the Indian context: the Praxis model (paralleling Dalit and Tribal theology) and Abhishiktananda's contemplative approach.

On Aquinas — theology as sacred science

Aquinas felt free to replace even Augustine's philosophical framework if a better one was available, "because philosophy is not itself necessary for salvation, but if theology uses a philosophy, it should use the best one available." Lonergan later judged this shift helpful on two counts: it gave medieval thinkers the quaestio method to reconcile patristic interpretations into coherent Summae, and it let theology address, through metaphysics, questions patristic Aristotelian logic had raised but not answered.

On the Praxis model — three questions

From where do theologians speak? — a situated standpoint, demanding a changed life, not just new ideas. Why do theologians speak? — to communicate faith in context and critically evaluate its prior expression. To whom do theologians speak? — to the "non-person," the poor denied basic dignity. Two Vatican responses (CDF, 1984 and 1986) appreciated the desire for liberation while warning against uncritical Marxist analysis and treating violence as anything but an absolute last resort.

Chapter 5Authority

The Criteria of Christian Theological Reflection

If so many theologies can be legitimate, where does pluralism end and heterodoxy begin? Raimundo Panikkar's sharpest question: "What is the criterion of the criterion?"

Three marks of Roman Catholic theology

Belief lived from within faith commitment, not detached science · a response to Revelation, carrying a character of givenness · an essential communitarian dimension — reflection of a person within the community.

The Church's threefold task

Martyria (witness) · Leitourgia (liturgy) · Diakonia (service to the poor, the sick, the imprisoned) — three tasks the whole People of God shares, not the clergy alone.

The Magisterium — how it is exercised

The Extraordinary Magisterium makes a solemn, infallible definition — an ecumenical council or an ex cathedra statement. The Ordinary Magisterium covers everything else: the Ordinary Universal (bishops worldwide, unanimously, teaching definitively or not) and the Ordinary Non-universal (individual bishops, episcopal conferences, and the Bishop of Rome's own ordinary teaching).

Eight parameters of the Catholic theological enterprise

  1. The knowability of God — rules out agnosticism.
  2. The partnership of faith and reason — rejects Fideism and Rationalism alike.
  3. Universality beyond sectarianism — Christ as the one Good News for all humanity.
  4. An essential communitarian context — sensus fidelium and ecumenism. India's own three Individual Churches live this criterion, not as an abstract principle but as daily ecclesial fact.
  5. Catholicity in time — real continuity with the past, without freezing into relativism.
  6. The Sacramental Principle — God's self-communication mediated through created realities.
  7. Revelation transmitted through Scripture and Tradition together.
  8. A real role for authority — the Magisterium, graded, each level given its due and proportionate respect.

Chapter 6Application

Theologizing in the Indian Context

When the Gospel met the Greeks, Romans and Syrians, it became Greek, Roman and Syrian theology. Did the same thing happen when the Gospel met India? Every earlier chapter built toward this question.

A. Three ecclesial families in India

"Hindu in culture, Christian in faith, Syrian in worship" (Placid Podipara). The St Thomas Christians, traditioned to 52 AD, gave rise to the Syro-Malabar (~5M, Major Archiepiscopal), the Syro-Malankara (reunited with Rome, 1930), and the Orthodox/Jacobite and Mar Thoma streams. The Latin Rite, from Xavier's arrival in 1542, is the largest today, 114 dioceses. The Protestant unions — CSI (1947) and CNI (1970) — complete the picture. Christians remain about 2.5% of India's population. The Catholic Church in India is a communion of three Individual Churches — Latin, Syro-Malabar, Syro-Malankara. Many Indian Churches "still carry the label of their foreign origin"; achieving a genuine fecundation between Gospel and Indian culture is named "the greatest challenge of all the Indian Churches."

Two streams converging

One stream works from culture, philosophy and religion: the St Thomas Christians and de Nobili's accommodatio; the Vedanta pioneers — Upadhyaya, Appasamy, Chenchaiah, Panikkar; the Christian Ashram Movement — Monchanin, Abhishiktananda, Bede Griffiths. The other works from socio-economic and political realities: Devanandan and M.M. Thomas on Christ and the new humanity; Kappen, Rayan and Soares-Prabhu's Indian Liberation Theology; Dalit Theology and Women's theology. The chapter's own convergence: these two streams are increasingly seen as related, not opposed, and an integrated Indian theology is slowly emerging.

B. Cultural, philosophical, religious horizons

A cosmotheandric view of reality — cosmic, divine and human as one. Human life understood through the four purusharthas (artha, kama, dharma, moksha) and four ashramas (brahmachari, grahastha, vanaprastha, sanyasi). Six classical darshanas: Nyaya, Vaisheshika, Sankhya, Yoga, Mimamsa, and Vedanta — the last, in its Advaita form, teaching Brahman alone as fully real, realized as Saccidananda, Being-Consciousness-Bliss.

Pioneers used this philosophical inheritance as an ancilla theologiae: Upadhyaya on Vedanta and the Trinity, Appasamy on Bhakti and Sat-Chit-Ananda, Sundar Singh's mystical "heart over head," Chenchaiah's Christ as the New Man through integral Yoga, Chakkarai's Christology-Pneumatology.

C. Socio-economic and political realities

Real industrial progress alongside deepening inequality — India ranked 128th of 174 countries on social development even while its middle class tripled. Caste discrimination persists despite constitutional abolition. The Constitution's "secular" means equal respect for all religions, not their absence — a character under real threat from Hindutva ideology, which the book calls "a very dangerous ideology" pushing toward a "Hindu Rashtra."

Dalit Theology — the cry of Dalit Christians, doubly discriminated: denied state benefits reserved for Hindu Dalits, and still facing prejudice inside parts of the Church. It deliberately rejects the upper-caste philosophical language of the earlier pioneers, drawing instead on Dalit "little traditions" — myths, stories, folklore, rituals — for a theology of liberation already implicit within them, centred on the Crucified and Risen Jesus.

"Where do we find the God of the Bible today? Is He not with the Dalits? Yes, He is the God of the Dalits."

D. Convergence, not resolution

Interreligious dialogue has shifted from debating doctrines toward the "dialogue of life" — shared concern for poverty, illiteracy, unemployment, women's issues, the environment. Inculturation remains vital but cannot be top-down "mere translation"; it happens naturally when the Church becomes genuinely involved in people's real lives. Signs of hope: the Indian Theological Association, journals such as Jeevadhara, Vidyajyoti and Bible Bhashyam, and growing theology departments in secular universities.
